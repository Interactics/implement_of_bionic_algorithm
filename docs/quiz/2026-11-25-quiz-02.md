# Quiz 02 · Control architecture & the training collapse (2026-11-25)

Covers: [control architecture](../control-architecture.md), [long training run](../devlog/2026-11-25-long-training.md), [ADR 0005](../adr/0005-stabilize-rl-training.md).

---

**Q1. Two loops.** Our controller has an outer loop and an inner loop. What runs in each, at what rate, and why not let the policy output torques directly at 200 Hz?

> [!success]- Answer
> The outer loop is the policy at 50 Hz, which outputs joint setpoints and a root wrench. The inner loop is the PD servo at 200 Hz, turning setpoints into torques. The PD loop gives fast, stiff joint-level stabilization for free, so the policy only has to learn slower, whole-body decisions (balance, timing). That makes learning much easier, and policies are more robust to timing and model errors. ReActor uses the same split.

**Q2. Feedforward vs feedback.** In q* = q_ref + 0.5·a, which part is feedforward and which is feedback? What happens with a = 0?

> [!success]- Answer
> q_ref is the feedforward part: what to do according to the plan, regardless of the current state. 0.5·a is the feedback correction, which the policy computes from the observed state. With a = 0 it's pure PD tracking of the IK reference, and the robot falls after about 0.74 s, because the reference contains no balance strategy.

**Q3. Reference preview.** Why does the observation include the *next* reference frame (in the heading frame), not just the robot's own state?

> [!success]- Answer
> The policy is a tracking controller, so it must know where to go, not just where it is. The heading frame makes the input independent of which way the robot faces in the world, so the same skill works in any direction.

**Q4. Reward as a control objective.** Map the reward terms onto optimal-control language.

> [!success]- Answer
> The tracking errors are the state cost (deviation from the reference). Torque, joint acceleration and action-rate penalties are the effort or smoothness cost. Survival plus early termination act as a constraint ("stay upright"). The wrench penalty prices the non-physical helper force.

**Q5. Reading the collapse.** Last night's KL spiked to over 100,000 and the clip fraction sat near 0.5, while the value loss stayed small. What does each signal tell you?

> [!success]- Answer
> A large KL means a single update changed the policy a lot. A clip fraction near 0.5 means half the samples were outside PPO's trust region on every update, so updates were too aggressive. A small value loss means the critic was fine, so the problem was the policy updates, not the value estimate.

**Q6. Three causes.** ADR 0005 names three causes that reinforce each other. Which one *triggers* the jump, which *allows* it, and which makes it *permanent*?

> [!success]- Answer
> - Allows: small batches (2k samples) with a fixed learning rate give noisy gradients and big steps.
> - Triggers: the unbounded squared-error reward, where one spinning step costs −53 and creates outlier advantages.
> - Permanent: silent action clipping. Once the mean is past ±1, the behaviour no longer changes, so there's no gradient back.

**Q7. Why an exp kernel?** Why `w·exp(−err²/σ²)` instead of simply clipping `−w·err²` at some minimum?

> [!success]- Answer
> The exp kernel is bounded in [0, w] but still smooth everywhere, so the policy can tell "bad" from "very bad". A clipped term is flat beyond the clip, so it gives zero gradient there. σ sets the error scale that still counts as "close".
